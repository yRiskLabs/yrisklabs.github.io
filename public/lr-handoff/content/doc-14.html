Parameterisation and Risk Management of Curve's LLAMMA Markets

Curve’s LlamaLend V1 markets are permissionless: any actor can deploy a market via the factory without prior approval. This openness makes market creation both accessible and high-stakes, as deployment choices—particularly oracle design and risk parameters—directly influence solvency, liquidation externalities, and borrower outcomes. Responsible market deployment, therefore, requires empirical stress testing of collateral assets across representative regimes (e.g., trends, volatility clustering, sharp drawdowns) and systematic evaluation of parameter sensitivity prior to launch.

These requirements motivated a design change in LlamaLend V2, where new market deployments are subject to governance approval, reflecting the need for domain expertise in market configuration. The LLAMMA simulator (“LlammaSimulator”), which was already available with LlamaLend V1, was developed to support this process by replaying historical price paths, instantiating a LLAMMA-style liquidation AMM, and quantifying loss and discount metrics under candidate parameterizations.

LLAMMA: Liquidation AMM Mechanism

We briefly summarize LLAMMA, the underlying mechanism we are modelling in the simulation.

The Liquidation AMM (LLAMMA) is a continuous liquidation mechanism designed to replace discrete, trigger-based liquidations with a smooth, price-contingent process. It operates over two assets: collateral Y (e.g., ETH) and debt X (e.g., crvUSD), with prices expressed in units of X/Y. The mechanism is parameterized around an external oracle price p0​ and a liquidation band defined by lower and upper thresholds p↓​<p↑​.

Band Structure and Inventory Regimes


LLAMMA partitions the price space into three regimes:

  1. Zone Y p0​>p↑​ : The AMM cell holds only collateral Y. In this regime, liquidation is inactive: the position is sufficiently overcollateralized.

  2. Zone X&Y p↓​≤p0​≤p↑​: The AMM holds a mixture of X and Y. Trades inside the cell gradually convert collateral into stablecoin as the price deteriorates, implementing partial liquidation.

  3. Zone X p0​<p↓​: The AMM holds only stablecoin X, corresponding to full liquidation.

This structure ensures that liquidation intensity increases smoothly as the oracle price moves deeper into the band, avoiding sudden jumps in position state.

Price-Dependent AMM Invariant

The core design choice of LLAMMA is to make the AMM invariant explicitly dependent on the oracle price p0​. Let X and Y denote the current reserves in the band. The invariant takes the schematic form

(X+α(p0​))(Y+β(p0​))=I,

where I is constant along a trade, and the shift terms α(p0​), β(p0​) are monotonic functions of p0​ that govern the transition between pure-X and pure-Y states.

In Curve’s reference construction, the effective internal buy and sell prices of the cell, p↑cell(p0​) and p↓cell(p0​), are chosen such that:

  • they coincide with p0​ at the band edges,

  • they move away from p0​ outside the band,

  • and they ensure arbitrage consistency with the external market.

A convenient parameterization expresses the band width via a single amplification parameter A, with

p↑​p↓​​=AA−1​,

which controls how aggressively inventory shifts as price moves across the band.

Economic Interpretation

LLAMMA can be interpreted as embedding the borrower’s collateral into a price-conditioned AMM trajectory. Instead of liquidating a position at a single trigger price, collateral is continuously swapped into stablecoin as the oracle price declines. This achieves three objectives simultaneously:

  • smoother liquidation paths,

  • reduced liquidation externalities,

  • and endogenous price impact through the AMM itself.

Crucially, LLAMMA defines liquidation not as a discrete event, but as a state-dependent trading process, fully determined by (p0​,A,p↓​,p↑​).

Simulation Framework

The LlammaSimulator implements a path-replay simulation of the LLAMMA liquidation mechanism under historical price dynamics. Given a time series of market prices, the simulator deterministically replays the evolution of a LLAMMA-style banded AMM, tracking inventory traversal, fee accumulation, and portfolio value over time. The objective is to quantify pathwise deterioration of a representative LLAMMA position under realistic volatility regimes and to summarize this deterioration using tail-risk statistics suitable for parameter comparison and risk calibration.

Price Input and Preprocessing

The simulator takes as input a historical OHLCV time series sampled at a fixed resolution (e.g. one-minute candles). Each observation is of the form (ti​,Oi​,Hi​,Li​,Ci​,Vi​), where ti​ denotes the timestamp and Hi​,Li​ define the intraperiod price range.

It is key to have a sufficiently long period of price data; we settle on 4 years’ worth of data on a 1-minute timeframe, as this historically represents a crypto cycle, meaning shows a set of diverse market conditions.

Note: The data we feed to the simulation should be close to what the market will see on-chain.

Data Availability Issues

When an asset lacks sufficient historical price data to support direct simulation (typically < 4 years of minute-level OHLCV), we extend the framework using proxy-based path construction with explicit uncertainty accounting. Assets are handled under two cases.

If partial history is available, we compute scale-free return statistics (annualized volatility, kurtosis, jump frequency, and first-order autocorrelation) on the overlapping window between the new asset and a set of candidate reference assets. These metrics are normalized and compared in feature space, and the reference asset with the minimal distance is selected as the closest proxy. The new asset’s observed price path is retained where available, and its missing pre-history is filled using the proxy asset’s return path aligned to the same sampling frequency.

If no usable history exists, the asset is directly assigned to a conservative reference class (e.g., high-volatility governance tokens), and the full simulated path is derived from that reference.

In both cases, proxy use introduces model risk. We therefore compute an explicit uncertainty score based on data coverage and proxy dissimilarity, and apply this as a multiplicative buffer to the resulting liquidation and loan discounts. This ensures that parameter recommendations remain conservative under data scarcity and are relaxed only as native price history accumulates.

Oracle Construction

LLAMMA liquidation dynamics depend on an oracle price rather than the raw market price. In the simulator, the oracle price pt​ is constructed as an exponential moving average (EMA) of intraperiod mid-prices:
Mi​=21​(Hi​+Li​),

pi​=αi​Mi​+(1−αi​)pi−1​,αi​=1−2−Δti​/Texp​,
where Texp​ is the EMA half-life (in seconds) and Δti​ is the elapsed time since the previous observation. This oracle smoothing introduces inertia into band boundaries and fee dynamics, reflecting the fact that LLAMMA liquidation thresholds evolve continuously rather than instantaneously with spot prices.

Effective Price Bounds and External Friction

At each timestep, the simulator evaluates whether the LLAMMA position would be arbitraged given the candle’s high–low range. To approximate execution frictions external to LLAMMA (e.g., routing costs, taker fees, residual slippage), the raw bounds are widened via an external fee parameter ϕ:

Hieff​=Hi​(1−ϕ),Lieff​=Li​(1+ϕ).

Trades are triggered only if these effective bounds cross the current AMM price, ensuring that simulated rebalancing corresponds to economically viable arbitrage opportunities.

LLAMMA State Initialization

Each simulation run represents a single hypothetical loan position. At the start of the run, a fixed quantity of collateral (normalized to one unit) is deposited across a discrete set of liquidation bands above the current oracle price. The number of bands (range_size) and their geometric spacing are determined by the amplification parameter (A), consistent with LLAMMA’s band construction.

This initial state represents a freshly opened position prior to any adverse price movement. All subsequent changes in portfolio value arise endogenously from price evolution, band traversal, and fees.

Deterministic Band Traversal

For each candle, the simulator performs the following steps:

  1. Oracle update: The oracle price pt​ is updated using the EMA rule.

  2. Target price mapping: The effective high and low prices are mapped into LLAMMA’s internal price space, accounting for band-specific fees and oracle-relative distortions.

  3. Arbitrage-driven rebalancing: If the effective high exceeds the current AMM price, the simulator deterministically trades upward, selling collateral and moving inventory toward higher bands. If the effective low falls below the AMM price, it trades downward, buying collateral and moving inventory toward lower bands.

  4. Cross-band execution: Trades proceed band by band. Within each band, the LLAMMA invariant is respected exactly; if a band is exhausted, the active band index is updated and trading continues until the target price is reached or no further arbitrage is possible.

This procedure produces a fully deterministic inventory path conditional on the price series and parameter set.

Fee Dynamics

Each trade is subject to an effective fee that combines multiple components:

  • a base fee, representing static protocol costs;

  • an oracle-movement fee, increasing with recent oracle volatility and decaying over time;

  • an optional distance-based fee, increasing with deviation from the oracle price.

At each band crossing, the applicable fee is applied asymmetrically depending on trade direction, reducing the effective target price when selling and increasing it when buying. Fees are accumulated implicitly through reduced inventory conversion, directly impacting portfolio value.

Portfolio Valuation

At any time t, the total portfolio value is expressed in the debt asset X by adiabatically converting all band inventories to X at the prevailing oracle price. This produces a single scalar value Vt(X)​independent of the current band configuration.

For a simulation window [t0​,t1​], the realized loss is defined
ℓ=1−Vt0​(X)​Vt1​(X)​​

Loss is reported as a unitless relative measure, defined as the fractional change in portfolio value in a fixed numeraire.

Sampling of Loan Windows

Rather than simulating a single contiguous trajectory, the simulator samples many random loan windows from the historical dataset. Each window is defined by:

  • a random start position within the dataset;

  • a fixed or bounded duration expressed in days.

For each window, the full simulation described above is executed independently, producing one loss observation. This sampling scheme approximates the distribution of outcomes faced by loans initiated at arbitrary points in time.

Tail-Loss Aggregation

Given a collection of loss observations (ℓ1​,…,ℓS​), the simulator focuses on the upper tail of the distribution. Losses are sorted in descending order, and the reported statistic is the mean of the worst m observations:

ESm/S​=m1​∑i=1mℓ(i)​

This tail-average loss is an empirical analogue of Expected Shortfall and is used as the primary objective for parameter comparison. It emphasizes adverse but plausible scenarios, aligning with the risk-management goals of liquidation parameterization.

LLAMMA Simulator V2 

The LlammaLend V2 simulator preserves the core of the original LLAMMA simulator. The primary changes are architectural refactoring, a revised fee simulation model, and the introduction of explicit stress amplification mechanisms.

Worst Day Scaling

A key new feature in the V2 simulator is worst-day scaling, an optional stress-testing mode that amplifies adverse price movements within sampled windows.

In practice, worst-day scaling is enabled by selecting the V2 mode (is_v2=True), which switches the price loader from GenericPriceHistoryLoader (raw replay) to VolatilityPriceHistoryLoader (drawdown-rescaled windows) in the experiment runners (e.g., see calculation.py simulate_fee / simulate_A).

When enabled, the simulator first scans the full OHLCV dataset to identify the maximum intraperiod drawdown—defined as the largest peak-to-trough decline from high Hi​ to low Li​​ over any window of a fixed duration. During simulation, instead of replaying a randomly sampled price window verbatim, the sampled window is rescaled so that its internal drawdown matches the worst observed drawdown.


Concretely, prices within the window are transformed via an affine scaling around the window’s maximum price, leaving the local structure of returns intact while increasing the amplitude of adverse movements. The resulting path preserves temporal ordering and relative price dynamics but enforces a worst-case drawdown magnitude.

Worst-day scaling is intended to emulate more severe but historically anchored stress scenarios, particularly for assets whose realized volatility during the evaluation horizon may underrepresent plausible downside risk. It is best interpreted as a conditional stress test rather than a probabilistic forecast. Analysts should note that this transformation alters the effective return distribution of the asset: while relative intrawindow dynamics are preserved. It can be useful for assets with shorter price history (i.e., that experience one or two extreme events).

Market Parameterisation Experiments

Using the simulation framework described above, we perform targeted parameter sweeps to identify economically and risk-efficient LLAMMA configurations. 

Configurable Parameters

The simulator parameters can be grouped into three categories:

  • price data inputs (historical OHLC series and preprocessing choices),

  • contract parameters (e.g., A, fee, oracle settings),

  • behavioural assumptions (e.g., range size, liquidation duration, external friction)

  • simulation controls (e.g., sampling, tail level, seed).

We now discuss each category in detail.

Price Data Inputs

We typically recommend obtaining 4 years' worth of 1-min price data from a trustworthy data source that reasonably represents the collateral debt relationship. For bluechip assets and large-cap, alt-coins, Binance is often a good resource. For instance, to parameterize the tBTC-crvUSD market, the BTC-USDT feed was used.

For additional data, one can load the existing inverted by setting add_reverse to false. We generally do not enable this if not needed, to avoid assuming symmetry in price action for up and down movements.

Data Availability Issues

When an asset has insufficient price history to run the 4-year, 1-minute replay, we treat this as explicit model risk and switch to a proxy-and-buffer procedure. Choosing the right asset still requires expert judgement and careful qualitative consideration of assets selected.

  • Case 1 — Partial history (< 4 years). Let the asset have returns rt(a)​ on its available window. We compare it to candidate references j∈R on the common overlapping window using a scale-free feature vector
    f(x)=(σann​(x), κ(x), π3σ​(x), ρ1​(x)),
    where σann​ is annualized realized vol, κ kurtosis, π3σ​ jump frequency Pr(∣r∣>3σ), and ρ1​ ACF(1). After z-scoring features across candidates, we select the closest proxy j∗=argminj∈R​ ∣f~(a)−f~(j)∣2​.
    We then construct a synthetic pre-history by splicing the asset’s observed segment with the proxy’s return dynamics for the missing period (returns-based, hence scale-free), yielding an effective 4-year series for the replay.

  • Case 2 — No usable history. We cannot compute f(a), so we choose a conservative reference class (e.g., high-vol governance-token basket) and treat uncertainty as maximal by construction.

Uncertainty buffer (both cases). We assign an uncertainty score U∈[0,1] combining (i) similarity error (distance in feature space) and (ii) coverage shortfall (how far we are from 4 years). This score drives conservative parameter uplifts, e.g.
liq_discountfinal=liq_discountsim×(1+λU),

loan_discountfinal=loan_discountsim×(1+λU),
with λ a governance-chosen conservatism factor. As more native history accrues, it decreases mechanically (coverage increases and similarity can be re-estimated), and parameters can be tightened accordingly.

Contract Parameter

Contract parameters are values defined by deployed contracts (immutable or governance-controlled) that structurally determine LLAMMA and oracle behavior. These parameters include:

  • A: the amplification coefficient or also known as the bandwidth factor) defines the density of the liquidity and band size. It can be looked onchain within the LLAMMA Contract (e.g., see sDOLA-crvUSD Market LLAMMA contract under the public method ‘A’).

  • fee: the base fee charged in LLAMMA to arbitrageurs and paid to the borrower. It can be looked onchain within the LLAMMA Contract (e.g., see sDOLA-crvUSD Market LLAMMA contract under the public method ‘fees’).

  • dynamic_fee_multiplier: is the hard-coded sensitivity of the LLAMMA AMM fee to deviations between the oracle price and the in-band price, used to penalize rapid or out-of-band price moves and protect the AMM from oracle jumps. It is not configurable at deployment or by governance and fixed in the AMM code as a ¼ coefficient and can be found in the LLAMMA contract  under get_dynamic_fee().

  • Texp: the exponential moving-average window used by the external price feed consumed by LLAMMA. It should be read from the underlying Oracle contract; if multiple pools are chained, we recommend using the shortest MA window (smallest ma_time / exp_ma_time) as it governs the fastest price response. The relevant pool address is exposed on-chain via the Oracle’s public pool method, and the MA parameter can be read directly from that pool contract (e.g., see sDOLA-crvUSD Market Oracle contract under the public method ‘pool’ and within the pool contract under exp_ma_time).

Behavioural Parameters

Behavioural assumptions are analyst-chosen assumptions about liquidation, arbitrage, and liquidity placement that are not enforced on-chain but materially affect tail losses and stress outcomes. These parameters include:

  • min_loan_days/max_loan_days: bound the assumed liquidation duration in the simulation. Each run samples a window within this range (typically between 30 to 60 minutes); longer windows typically increase price exposure and tail losses, shorter windows approximate faster liquidations and reduce losses.

  • range_size: the number of bands used to initialize a position in the simulation. For conservative parameterization, this is set to 4, as it concentrates liquidity into the fewest allowed bands, reducing price-absorption capacity and causing bands to deplete faster, which maximizes worst-case (liquidation-style) losses. Larger ranges spread liquidity more evenly and mechanically lower tail losses.

  • external_fee: the transaction cost paid by arbitrageurs when routing trades outside LLAMMA (e.g., DEX fees, aggregator fees, and gas). In the simulation, it is modeled as an exogenous spread that reduces the effective price improvement arbitrageurs can capture, thereby weakening arbitrage pressure and increasing residual inventory loss inside the AMM. Higher external fees delay rebalancing and raise worst-case losses; setting it to zero assumes frictionless arbitrage and yields a lower-bound loss estimate.

Simualtion Parameters

Simulation parameters are technical settings that control how losses are estimated (sampling, tails, determinism) without changing the underlying economic mechanism. These parameters include:

  • samples: the number of Monte Carlo liquidation paths drawn from the historical price series; higher values reduce estimation error, while the deterministic mode evaluates all admissible windows without resampling.

  • n_top_samples: the number of worst-loss realizations averaged to compute Expected Shortfall, controlling the granularity of tail-risk estimation for a given sample size.

  • tails.liquidation: the tail probability used to estimate the liquidation discount, corresponding to the Expected Shortfall of the worst α fraction of simulated losses.

  • tails.loan: the tail probability used to estimate the loan discount, targeting a deeper tail than liquidation to provide an additional solvency buffer at loan origination.

  • seed: the random seed used for Monte Carlo sampling to ensure reproducibility of stochastic experiments.

Finding the Fee Parameter

The internal fee controls the trade-off between liquidation efficiency and inventory protection. Low fees allow inventory to traverse bands with minimal friction but expose the AMM to adverse selection during volatile price movements. High fees, conversely, reduce adverse selection but increase cumulative loss by inhibiting timely re-centering.

To identify an appropriate fee level, we perform a one-dimensional sweep over candidate fee values while holding all other parameters fixed. For each fee, the simulator estimates the tail-average loss over randomly sampled loan windows. The resulting loss curve is typically convex: losses decrease initially as fees provide protection, then increase once excessive friction dominates.


Source: LlammaSimulator V2 - ETH/USDT Fee Experiment

The fee is selected at the minimum of this loss curve, corresponding to the point where marginal protection from higher fees is exactly offset by marginal execution inefficiency. This criterion directly aligns the fee choice with observed tail risk rather than average-case performance.

Finding the Amplification Parameter (A)

The amplification parameter (A) determines the geometric spacing of liquidation bands and thus governs how finely collateral is distributed across price levels. Smaller (A) produces coarse bands with abrupt inventory transitions, while larger (A) yields finer ladders at the cost of increased traversal and fee accumulation.

Given a fixed fee (as determined above), we perform a sweep over (A) and evaluate two complementary quantities:

  1. the tail-average loss, capturing realized deterioration under dynamic price paths; and

  2. a liquidation discount proxy, which adjusts the loss by the static geometry of the band ladder and reflects the disadvantage of unwinding deeper bands first.


Source: LlammaSimulator V2 - ETH/USDT A Experiment

The optimal amplification A∗ is chosen at the minimum of the liquidation discount curve (orange), representing the balance between volatility absorption and re-centering cost. The liquidation discount is then read off the loss curve (blue) at this same A∗, ensuring that both parameters are internally consistent and jointly calibrated to empirical tail behavior.

Finding the Liquidation Discount

The liquidation discount is used to discount the collateral for calculating the recoverable value upon liquidation at the current market price.

Given that we are sampling a random segment of the historical sample data, we are not necessarily covering every scenario of the historical data. Sampling is necessary as we are conducting parameter sweeps across parameters, which in turn becomes costly.

Yet, for a fixed A and fixed fee, key parameters are already determined, meaning sweeps are not necessary anymore. This allows us to switch to deterministic sampling. Hence, we recommend that once the fee and A are determined, re-run without sampling and use the resulting loss as the market liquidation discount.

Generally, we are looking at the top 1% worst losses resulting from the simulation, which, for 4-year 1-min data on a 1h time window, roughly corresponds to 200 cases.

Finding the Loan Discount

The loan discount is the percentage used to discount the collateral for calculating the maximum borrowable amount when creating a loan.

Loan discount has been in the V1 version, historically just received a 3 percent margin to the liquidation_discount. We instead recommend defining the loan discount margin endogenously from the simulated loss distribution. Specifically, we set the margin based on the gap between two extreme quantiles of deterministic losses: for example, the difference between the 1% tail and the 0.25% tail (approximately the worst 200 versus the worst 50 scenarios). This margin captures the slope of the extreme tail, providing a statistically grounded buffer that accounts for how rapidly losses escalate beyond the liquidation threshold.

This construction ensures that newly opened loans remain solvent even if market conditions deteriorate from severe to extreme, while avoiding arbitrary buffers that may be either insufficient or overly restrictive.

Note: Currently we maintain to regardless of identified margin, a minimum margin requirement of 3% for uncorrelated assets and 0.5% fo correlated assets.

Online Monitoring of Loan & Liquidation discount 

Once a market is deployed, core structural parameters of the LLAMMA mechanism are fixed and cannot or are unlikely to change. In this case, the LlammaSimulator can be repurposed from a parameter-search tool into a deterministic monitoring framework that continuously re-estimates the implied loan and liquidation discounts under evolving market conditions.

Objective

The objective of online monitoring is to detect whether the risk buffers implied by recent market dynamics remain aligned with the discounts currently enforced on-chain. Rather than re-optimizing (A) or fees, the framework recomputes liquidation and loan discounts using the deployed configuration and compares them to the active parameters, providing an evidence-based signal for governance review. Deterministic Simulation Update

At each monitoring run, the simulator executes a deterministic path-replay over a fixed rolling evaluation horizon using the current market configuration:

  1. Price data loading. Historical OHLC data are loaded with sufficient warmup to ensure oracle convergence. All timestamps and evaluation boundaries are defined centrally to guarantee consistency across runs.

  2. Rolling window evaluation. Losses are computed over a deterministic grid of rolling windows (fixed window length and stride) covering the evaluation horizon. This replaces random sampling and ensures reproducibility.

  3. Incremental computation. Previously computed windows are reused. Only new windows since the last run are simulated, while windows falling outside the horizon are discarded. If the market configuration changes, the cache is invalidated, and the horizon is recomputed in full.

  4. Tail aggregation. Window losses are aggregated into tail-average loss statistics, from which implied liquidation and loan discounts are derived using the same mapping as in initial parameterization.

Decision Rule

The monitoring output consists of updated estimates of liq_discount and loan_discount. These are compared to the corresponding on-chain parameters:

  • If deviations exceed a predefined tolerance (i.e., currently 1% difference), a governance update is triggered.

The system does not perform automatic parameter updates; it provides a structured, reproducible risk signal to support governance decisions.

Limitation and Future Research

In this section, we discuss the key limitations and areas for future research:

  • Unseen tail events: The framework is inherently historical and path-based: it cannot guarantee coverage of extreme regimes that have not occurred in the observed data (e.g., novel market structure failures, sudden delistings, protocol-specific exploits). As a result, estimated tail losses may understate true worst-case risk. A future improvement to the methodology utilises generative pricing models informed by the statistical properties of the time series. This will allow us to amplify specific properties (e.g., jump frequency or volatility) of the series and enable us to test for scenario more extreme than have been observed historically in the data.

  • Regime and structural breaks. The methodology assumes stationarity of return dynamics. Fundamental shifts in an asset’s behavior—such as changes in utility, liquidity venues, tokenomics, regulatory status, or holder composition—can invalidate historical similarity and cause proxy-based estimates to become unreliable. With the online monitoring framework, we attempt to mitigate this, yet in the future, more sophisticated regime detection may be a promising area of future research.

  • Proxy misclassification risk. For assets with partial or no history, risk parameters rely on reference assets selected via statistical similarity. Even with conservative buffers, an incorrect proxy choice can lead to systematic mis-estimation of liquidation and loan risk.

These limitations imply that results should be interpreted as risk-informed lower bounds under modeled conditions, not as guarantees of solvency. Continuous monitoring, periodic re-parameterization, and governance oversight remain necessary as market conditions evolve.

Conclusion

This work formalizes a reproducible, tail-risk–focused methodology for LLAMMA market parameterization, combining path-replay simulation, conservative stress testing, and explicit uncertainty handling. By grounding fee, amplification, and discount choices in empirical worst-case behavior—while retaining governance oversight and continuous monitoring—the framework provides a robust lower-bound assessment of liquidation risk that can be systematically updated as market conditions and data availability evolve.
