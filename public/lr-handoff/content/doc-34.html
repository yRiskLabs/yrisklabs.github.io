[LlamaLend]: sUSDf Collateral Onboarding Assessment

Executive Summary

This report evaluates sUSDf as proposed collateral for an Ethereum mainnet Curve LlamaLend market with crvUSD as the borrow asset. Falcon describes sUSDf as the yield-bearing version of USDf, minted when USDf is deposited into Falcon’s ERC-4626 vaults, with value accruing through the sUSDf-to-USDf exchange rate.

The collateral risk chain is therefore:

sUSDf→USDf→Falcon reserve / redemption / governance / custody / strategy stack

sUSDf inherits the core USDf risks: reserve quality, redemption access, KYC dependence, issuer discretion, off-chain strategy execution, custody, and governance. It also adds wrapper-specific risks: ERC-4626 accounting, PPS correctness, vault upgradeability, reward-distribution mechanics, and liquidity of the staked wrapper.


1. Asset Fundamental Characteristics

1.1 Asset

sUSDf is the yield-bearing wrapper for USDf, Falcon Finance’s overcollateralized synthetic dollar. USDf can be minted by users who have completed Falcon’s onboarding requirements and have deposited approved collateral. For approved stablecoin collateral, USDf is minted through the classic mint process at a 1:1 ratio. For approved non-stablecoin collateral, users may use either the classic mint or the “innovative mint” process, both of which rely on overcollateralization so that the value of deposited collateral exceeds the value of minted USDf.

The underlying USDf asset is not backed by a single collateral type. Falcon accepts stablecoins, non-stablecoin cryptoassets, and real-world-asset exposures such as tokenized gold, tokenized stocks, tokenized U.S. Treasuries, and tokenized corporate credit. Falcon’s collateral framework screens eligible assets based on exchange listing quality, liquidity, open interest, funding-rate stability, and broader market conditions. This makes USDf a synthetic dollar backed by a diversified collateral and strategy stack rather than a narrow fiat-reserve stablecoin.

After minting, collateral is routed through Falcon’s custody and trading infrastructure. Falcon uses third-party custodians and off-exchange settlement infrastructure to support centralized-exchange activity while reducing direct exchange-custody exposure. Falcon then deploys collateral into yield-generating strategies. The relevant strategy set includes positive and negative funding-rate arbitrage, collateral or native asset staking, cross-exchange arbitrage, spot/perpetuals arbitrage, statistical arbitrage, options-based strategies, and liquidity provision.

sUSDf sits one layer above this USDf system. Falcon describes sUSDf as the yield-bearing version of USDf, minted when USDf is deposited into Falcon’s ERC-4626 vault. The amount of sUSDf received depends on the prevailing sUSDf-to-USDf exchange rate. As Falcon generates yield, newly minted USDf is allocated into the sUSDf vault, increasing the amount of USDf redeemable per unit of sUSDf over time.

1.2 Architecture

Falcon’s architecture has two layers relevant to this market. The first is the USDf minting and reserve-management layer. The second is the sUSDf vault layer, where USDf is deposited to receive a yield-bearing ERC-4626 share token.

USDf

Minting

Users who have passed KYC requirements are allowed to mint USDf by depositing approved collateral assets. Users are allowed to mint USDf through two methods: classic mint or innovative mint. Suppose a user chooses to mint using an approved stablecoin; the classic mint method is used, where USDf is returned at a 1:1 ratio. If a user chooses to mint using an approved non-stablecoin asset, either the classic or innovative mint method can be used. In both methods, USDf is not returned at a 1:1 ratio. For innovative mint, the user selects a lockup period, strike price, and capital efficiency level, which determines the amount of USDf eligible to be minted. For classic mint, the user simply deposits an accepted asset and receives USDf. These methods always utilize an overcollateralization ratio, making sure that the backing collateral is always greater in value than the minted USDf. Users can also acquire USDf from secondary marketplaces.


This diagram illustrates the minting flow of USDf tokens, showing interactions between off-chain and on-chain components. Off-chain, the user submits an order via the Falcon UI, which is validated, hashed, and signed by the Minter/Custodian. The signed order is sent on-chain to the PreCollateralizedMinter contract via preCollateralizedMint(), which then calls the USDf token contract’s mint() function to issue tokens to the user.

Reserve Architecture

Falcon routes deposited collateral into third-party custodians and off-exchange settlement infrastructure. According to Falcon Finance’s Transparency Page, Ceffu and Fireblocks are key custody providers, yet with the majority of Reserves (~95%) being held in their own Multisig wallet.

sUSDf Vault Architecture

sUSDf is created when USDf is deposited into Falcon’s ERC-4626 vault. Users receive sUSDf according to the prevailing sUSDf-to-USDf exchange rate. Falcon calculates generated yield daily, mints new USDf from that yield, and deposits a portion of the newly minted USDf into the sUSDf vault. This increases the amount of USDf redeemable per unit of sUSDf over time.

Redemptions

Falcon distinguishes between sUSDf unstaking and USDf redemption. sUSDf can be unstaked into USDf immediately, whereas USDf redemption into underlying collateral is subject to a 7-day cooldown. Falcon also documents a brief daily lock window around yield calculation, during which staking and unstaking interactions may be queued.

Peg Stability Mechanism

USDf’s peg mechanism relies on three linked stabilizers: active hedging, overcollateralization, and arbitrage. Falcon states that collateral deposited to mint USDf is managed through delta-neutral and market-neutral strategies to reduce directional exposure to the underlying collateral assets. In parallel, USDf minted against non-stablecoin collateral is overcollateralized, so the collateral value is intended to exceed the value of USDf issued. The market peg is then supported by arbitrage: when USDf trades above $1, KYC-approved users can mint USDf and sell it externally; when USDf trades below $1, users can buy discounted USDf and redeem it for collateral value through Falcon. For sUSDf, this peg mechanism matters indirectly: sUSDf accrues value in USDf terms, but its final collateral value still depends on USDf remaining close to its intended dollar value.

1.3 Tokenomics

sUSDf tokenomics are primarily defined by its vault-share mechanics. Users deposit USDf into Falcon’s sUSDf ERC-4626 vault and receive sUSDf based on the prevailing sUSDf-to-USDf exchange rate. sUSDf is therefore not designed to remain fixed at 1.00 USDf. Instead, its value should increase relative to USDf as yield is allocated to the vault.

Yield accrues through the sUSDf-to-USDf exchange rate. Falcon calculates generated yield daily across its strategies, mints new USDf from that yield, and deposits a portion of the newly minted USDf into the sUSDf vault. This increases the amount of USDf redeemable per unit of sUSDf.

The source of sUSDf yield results from trading strategies (see 1.2. Architecture under Yield Source) and depends on their success.

3. Technological Risk

3.1 Smart Contract Risk

Falcon’s official docs list the sUSDf contract and the sUSDf Staking Rewards Distributor on Ethereum mainnet. Falcon also states that USDf and sUSDf were audited by Zellic and Pashov, with no critical or high-severity vulnerabilities identified in either assessment. Falcon’s GitHub repository is not public, yet deployed contracts and documentation are available.

USDf is natively deployed on Ethereum, which has logic implemented via a TransparentUpgradeableProxy proxy pattern. USDf is also natively deployed on Binance Smart Chain, but has no proxy implementation. Users can bridge between Ethereum and Binance Smart Chain using a third-party application or Chainlink's CCIP. Finally, USDf can also be bridged to XRPL via Axelar.


USDf can be staked into sUSDf on Ethereum, which has logic implemented via a TransparentUpgradeableProxy proxy pattern. The sUSDf staking rewards distributor can be viewed here and does not have a proxy implementation. The contract for restaked USDf positions represented as ERC-721 NFTs can be viewed here and has a proxy implementation.

3.2 Bug bounty program

LlamaRisk’s earlier USDf review stated that Falcon planned to launch a bug bounty program within Q1 2026. However, as of this review, no public bug bounty or formal vulnerability-disclosure program covering USDf, sUSDf, the staking rewards distributor, or related oracle contracts could be independently verified.

3.3 Dependency Risk

Falcon uses collateral from USDf to generate yield in several ways, all of which bring some third-party risk.

On-chain liquidity strategies involve liquidity pools that rely on third-party protocols. This makes Falcon vulnerable to exploit of a partner protocol, which could result in a loss of funds. Falcon currently uses well-established on-chain liquidity pools. This includes Uniswap, Curve, and PancakeSwap on Ethereum and Binance Smart Chain. The liquidity pools are paired against core stable assets such as USDT, USDC, USD1, as well as smaller pools with frxUSD. The liquidity pools that Falcon uses introduce potential dependencies, as these liquidity pools are reliant on these on-chain protocols.

Native altcoin staking also brings in dependency risk. Falcon states that they use professional staking service providers, such as Luganodes, when staking altcoins. Altcoins are only staked if there is a negotiated zero lock-up period, allowing Falcon to swiftly exit positions in times of market volatility.

Falcon relies on two custodial solutions to house funds and utilize off-exchange settlement. Although these custodians bring third-party risk, the risk is minimized through diversification and multisig/MPC measures.

Minting functions for USDf occur entirely off-chain. Falcon states that they use an internal price oracle that integrates over 40 trading venues to calculate an internal fair price. This reduces the dependency risk by utilizing a diverse selection of data sources; however, the off-chain oracle process is not publicly available.

The integration of Chainlink CCIP bridging has a strong security record, but also brings additional risks as a third-party application.

4. Counterparty Risk

4.1 Governance and Regulatory Risk

sUSDf inherits the regulatory, reserve-management, and issuer-discretion risks of USDf because it is ultimately a yield-bearing claim on USDf. Falcon Digital Limited is the issuer-facing entity identified in Falcon’s terms, which are governed by British Virgin Islands law. Falcon states that it is not currently licensed by any regulatory authority. The USDf report further notes that Falcon obtained a BVI-law memorandum concluding, subject to its assumptions, that USDf is not an “investment” under SIBA and that Falcon, acting solely as issuer, would not require VASP registration. This conclusion remains dependent on the actual scope and location of Falcon’s activities, particularly where custody, transfer, minting, redemption, or issuer-related financial services are carried on from the BVI.

USDf reserve safeguards are primarily contractual and operational rather than imposed by a prudential regime. Falcon maintains voluntary overcollateralization for USDf minted against non-stable collateral, with asset-specific buffers adjusted for volatility, liquidity, and slippage. It also operates an on-chain insurance fund and publishes reserve information through a transparency dashboard and regular HT Digital collateral letters. However, the published collateral letters are point-in-time reports and explicitly do not verify ownership, control, or whether assets are encumbered. Reserve protection therefore depends on Falcon’s internal risk controls, custody arrangements, and ability to maintain clean title and liquidity across its reserve portfolio.

The reserve and redemption structure is permissioned. Minting and redemption require KYC/KYB, redemption is subject to a minimum size and a seven-day processing period, and Falcon retains broad discretion to apply restrictions, suspend accounts, monitor activity, or block transactions. Falcon’s terms also restrict U.S. persons and users in sanctioned or otherwise restricted jurisdictions. The USDf implementation includes an on-chain restriction mechanism that can prevent transfers involving addresses placed on a denylist. These controls may support sanctions compliance and incident response, but they mean issuer redemption is not an immediate, permissionless liquidation backstop.

The main regulatory and governance risk for sUSDf is therefore not only the legal status of the token itself, but the continued functioning of the underlying Falcon issuer, reserve-management, custody, compliance, and redemption framework. This risk is amplified by the heterogeneous nature of USDf reserve assets, which may include stablecoins, cryptoassets, and tokenized real-world assets with different custody, transferability, and regulatory characteristics. For LlamaLend, liquidation assumptions should rely on secondary-market liquidity rather than guaranteed issuer redemption, while ongoing monitoring should cover reserve disclosures, custody concentration, changes in access restrictions, and material changes to Falcon’s legal or regulatory status.

Operational Security Practices

Falcon Finance maintains a policy of discretion regarding specific operational security protocols to maximize protection. The protocol employs a hybrid operational model, with a dedicated core team that ensures agile and decisive management. Falcon has launched a governance framework to empower community participants to vote on protocol developments. Falcon enforces a robust management practice where at least 3 members of the trading team must work on each strategy, reducing the risk of a single member making an exploitable mistake. There are currently no timelocks implemented in core Falcon Finance contracts, meaning that administrative actions have no enforced delay.

5. LlamaLend-Specific Parameters

5.1 Oracle Design

The planned Oracle construction is proposed as follows:

PsUSDf/crvUSD​=PcrvUSD/USDC​PPSsUSDf/USDf​×PUSDf/USDC​​

Where:

  • PsUSDf/crvUSD​ = Final Oracle Price

  • PPSsUSDf/USDf​ = ERC-4626 Price Per Share Vault (0xc8cf6d7991f15525488b2a83df53468d682ba4b0)

  • PUSDf/USDC​ = StableSwapNG pool of USDf/USDC (0x72310DAAed61321b02B08A547150c07522c6a976)

  • PcrvUSD/USDC​ = Pegkeeper StableSwap NG USDC/crvUSD (0x4DEcE678ceceb27446b35C672dC7d61F30bAD69E)


Concern: Where the pool used in the collateral oracle is also a material liquidation venue, limited liquidity can create a self-reinforcing liquidation loop. A USDf sell-off lowers the pool price; positions then approach or cross liquidation thresholds, causing additional USDf collateral to be sold through the same pool or routing path. Those sales further deepen the imbalance and depress the oracle-relevant price, potentially triggering further liquidations before arbitrage capital can restore the peg. The risk is therefore not only insufficient exit capacity at a fixed price, but endogenous deterioration of the liquidation trigger itself as liquidation flow feeds back into the oracle market. The severity depends on the oracle design—particularly any EMA, external-price component, or pool weighting—and on whether liquidators can reliably access sufficiently deep alternative routes.

5.3 Exposure Limits

The USDf/crvUSD borrow-cap estimate was produced using the fresh-market sizing mode. Since this is treated as a fresh market, the method does not rely on observed borrower positions. Instead, it converts stressed executable liquidation capacity into a borrow-cap estimate using an assumed borrower LTV, a price-shock scenario, and a residual hard-liquidation pressure model.

The liquidity-capacity input is based on the USDf → crvUSD route. Route capacities are not summed, because different routes may share the same underlying liquidity. At a maximum accepted liquidation slippage of 1.4%, the observed route capacity was 0.739m USDf. This was divided by a 1.5× liquidity-contraction factor, giving a stressed executable capacity of 0.493m USDf.

The market parameters imply a maximum LTV of:

Lmax​=1−loan discount−2AN​

Using a loan discount of 1.9%, (A = 154), and (N = 4), this gives:

Lmax​=96.801

The hard-liquidation LTV proxy is:

Lhard​=1−liquidation discount=98.6

The borrower LTV assumption was set equal to maximum LTV. This is conservative but appropriate for this use case, because these markets are expected to be primarily used for looping strategies, where borrowers are likely to operate close to the maximum allowed LTV.

The empirical shock input was estimated as a 60-minute downside event shock and produced an empirical price shock of:

sempirical​=0.8924

However, the empirical shock alone is below the model-implied hard-liquidation trigger:

strigger​=1−Lhard​Lmax​​=1.8242

Since the empirical shock of 0.8924% does not breach this threshold, it does not create hard-liquidation pressure under the buffer model. To avoid falling back to a full-pressure assumption while still producing a binding cap-sizing scenario, the structural-trigger policy was used. This sets the sizing shock equal to the trigger shock plus a 25 bps margin:

ssizing​=strigger​+0.25

This produces a shocked price of 0.9744 and a stressed LTV of 98.85%. Under this scenario, the model estimates that 12.05% of the assumed borrower exposure becomes residual hard-liquidation pressure.

Soft-liquidation efficiency was set to 0%. This means the model does not credit soft liquidation with reducing hard-liquidation pressure over the short stress horizon. This is intentionally conservative: for a short 60-minute stress window, the analysis assumes that soft-liquidation execution may not reliably absorb the adverse move before hard-liquidation pressure has to be cleared through external USDf/crvUSD liquidity.

The resulting structural-trigger buffer-adjusted borrow cap is:

3.86m

The full-pressure cap is also reported as a conservative lower-bound diagnostic:

0.465m

The full-pressure cap represents the conservative bound obtained by assuming that the full residual borrower exposure must be liquidated through external liquidity, without applying the LTV-buffer adjustment that reduces pressure when only a fraction of positions breaches the hard-liquidation threshold.

This result should be interpreted as a structural-trigger buffer-adjusted cap, not as a full-pressure liquidation cap. The empirical shock was non-binding; the final sizing scenario intentionally uses the model-implied liquidation trigger plus margin to ensure that the cap reflects a buffer-breach stress without defaulting to the overly conservative full-pressure assumption. The gap between the buffer-adjusted cap and the full-pressure cap is driven by the fact that, under the structural-trigger scenario, only 12.05% of assumed borrower exposure is modeled as residual hard-liquidation pressure.

Recommendation

We recommend not listing, or, if really desired, a very cautious listing of sUSDf, with conservative risk parameters and a low borrow cap. Our key concerns include:

  • Deteriorating USDf peg quality, with a sustained increase in deviations from the intended $1 peg over recent months.

  • Limited liquidation capacity, where liquidity is relatively shallow for USDf and sUSDf

  • Oracle-liquidation feedback risk, as the proposed oracle relies on a liquidity venue that may also serve as a primary liquidation route, potentially amplifying market stress during adverse conditions.

  • Centralized redemption and governance dependencies, including KYC-gated redemptions, issuer discretion, custody concentration, and upgradeable contracts without timelock protections.

  • Complex reserve composition, where USDf value ultimately depends on a heterogeneous mix of crypto assets, tokenized RWAs, custodial arrangements, and active yield-generation strategies.
