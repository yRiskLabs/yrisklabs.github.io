CRV-long LlamaLend Recovery Pool Analysis

1. Purpose and objectives

The CRV-long LlamaLend market carries roughly $705K of bad debt: loans where the CRV collateral is worth less than the crvUSD owed, leaving the vault's lenders holding shares (cvcrvUSD) that can't be redeemed at face. Before April this debt had no path to resolution other than waiting for CRV to recover on its own.

michwill proposed a free-market alternative in CRV-long Llamalend market recovery: a small Curve pool that trades the impaired vault share against crvUSD, priced through a rate oracle pinned at pricePerShare / √2 (a ~29.3% floor discount). The pool lets anyone buy the share cheaply and then run an atomic liquidation that pays down a bad loan and redeems the share back out of the vault. If the share was bought cheaply enough, the trade clears a profit. The mechanism doesn't erase the loss. It makes paying it down a rational trade for outside capital instead of a charity nobody volunteers for.

The proposal frames this as "a recovery method with an option-like payoff, working as an investment for everyone who wants to participate." The DAO is invited but not required. The only thing it asked of governance was a gauge so the pool's liquidity providers could earn CRV emissions. By construction the whole thing is permissionless and runs without ongoing intervention once the gauge is set, which is a useful property rather than a goal in itself.

The design was put forward against two earlier-discussed alternatives that both spend protocol money directly:

  • a direct treasury repayment of the bad debt (Temp Check: Use Treasury Funds to Repay Bad Debt), and

  • the compensation-grant precedent from the Vyper exploit (Proposal to Recompensate LPs Affected by Curve Pool Exploit).

This report has two jobs. First, with the pool live for about six weeks and the gauge now weighted, check whether each thing the design set out to do is actually happening. Second, judge whether the approach is a viable, repeatable way to recover bad debt, specifically whether it can scale to the other impaired LlamaLend markets, which together carry roughly $1.22M more. The first is a goal-by-goal audit; the second is the question that decides whether this is a one-off fix or a template.

3. The goals

The pool aims to:

  1. Give stuck lenders a real exit, at a transparent discount.

  2. Reduce the bad debt by making liquidation profitable enough that it actually gets paid down.

  3. Fund deep pool liquidity through CRV emissions, enough yield to keep LPs in, at far lower cost to the DAO than a direct buyout.

  4. Open an option-like bet on CRV recovery to outside capital.

4. Goal-by-goal assessment

1. A real exit for stuck lenders

Two lenders have sold into the pool. They received $6,172 against $9,579 of face owed, a 35.6% realized discount. That's a touch worse than the 29.3% floor the rate oracle is supposed to guarantee, because the pool was leaning share-heavy when they sold, which pushes the price below the floor.

A larger exit can move the price hard, but the number that matters to a lender is what they lose against face value. A simulated $50K sell-down costs about 5.8% in slippage against spot now, down from 98% before emissions deepened the pool. Slippage against spot flatters the picture, though, because spot already trades well below face. Measured against face value, that same $50K exit is about a 38.5% haircut, and it has barely improved as the pool deepened, because roughly 29.3% of it is the structural √2 floor that depth can't remove. The chart shows both lines: slippage-vs-spot falls toward zero while discount-vs-face plateaus near 38%, and the gap between them is the floor. So the venue exists and the depth is real, but a lender still walks away about 38% below face. Adoption is tiny regardless. Most lenders are still holding.

2. Reduce the bad debt

Of 36 liquidations, 35 ran the full atomic recipe in a single transaction: flash-borrow crvUSD, buy the cheap share, liquidate the loan, redeem the share, repay the flash loan, keep the spread. Liquidators paid $52,314 of shortfall they would never have paid voluntarily, and walked away with roughly $55,675 gross before gas. The arbitrage is live and profitable, which is exactly the behavior the design needed to manufacture.

Cumulative shortfall paid by liquidators across the 36 events


On the protocol's balance sheet that shows up as a real dent. The live bad-debt mark fell from $757,509 to $705,195, and the $52K of improvement matches the shortfall the liquidators paid almost exactly, the clean signal that the pool, not a CRV price move, is what bent the curve. Per-share backing recovered to about 77%.

However every other position is too deep to be profitable. Of the $705K still outstanding, almost every position is missing 40 to 55% of its backing, while a liquidator only earns about 30 to 35% on the share discount, so the loss to close them outweighs the gain. None have been touched at today's CRV price, and the bad debt stopped falling the moment that one borrower was exhausted.

Shortfall/debt for every position vs the arb-profitability ceiling


So the curve didn't bend a little and keep going. It bent once, by the size of the single solvent position, and flattened. At constant CRV the bad debt sits at ~$705K and backing holds near 77%, not climbing toward 100%. The remaining 93% waits on a CRV recovery, not on more time.

4. Emissions subsidy: deep pool, cheap for the DAO

The emissions do two jobs at once; they pay LPs about 23.9% APR, and that yield pulled TVL from nothing to ~$460K. The pool is deep because the reward is attractive. They are also the DAO's entire cost. A direct buyout would cost the DAO a dollar for every dollar of bad debt retired. This route has so far spent $8,412 of emissions against $52,314 of bad debt retired, about $0.16 of subsidy per dollar cleared.

The emissions don't pay the shortfall. Liquidators pay it, funded by the discount that exiting lenders accept. The emissions only rent the pool depth that makes the arbitrage liquid. So the $0.16 is the DAO's subsidy cost per dollar cleared, and it's low precisely because the principal cost has been pushed onto arbitrageurs and the lenders who sell cheap.

Fees have covered only about 34% of the emissions bill over the window, and in most weeks far less. The pool came closest to paying for itself during one volume spike, when fees reached 88% of that week's emissions, then fell back below 10%.

5. A long call option-like bet for outside capital

The design allows outside capital to take a speculative position: buy the vault share at a discount, keep the floor value on the downside, and keep the upside if CRV climbs back toward the $0.96–$1.24 band where shares redeem at full face.

On the liquidity side, the bet is clearly populated: about 93% of pool TVL comes from addresses that aren't stuck lenders. On the share-holding side, the data is unambiguous. About 162 million shares have been bought out of the pool, roughly $203K of face value, and almost all of it was round-tripped straight back out by the arbitrage bot in the same transaction. Once you strip out those round-trips and any shares re-deposited as pool liquidity, what's left genuinely held in a wallet is 130,000 shares, about $163 of face value, spread across six addresses. That's 0.08% of everything bought. Nobody is holding the share as a standing option in any size that matters.

So the design's speculation venue exists in the form people will actually use, which is providing liquidity for the emissions yield. However the pool is carried by yield-farming LPs and the arbitrage bot, not by option-holders.

5. What to expect, holding CRV price and vote weight constant

Hold CRV near $0.21 and the gauge weight near 0.45%, and the headline is short: the arbitrage has essentially already done what it can. The tempting move is to read the $52K retired so far as a pace and extrapolate it to a full clear in about a year. That would be wrong, and goal 2 shows why. The bot didn't clear $52K and keep going. It cleared the one position whose shortfall sat inside the pool discount, ground it to dust, and ran out of profitable targets. There is no pace to carry forward.

Bad debt stalls near $705K. The ~$52K already retired is close to the whole of what the arb can reach at this price. The remaining 93% sits in positions too deep to liquidate at a profit, so absent a CRV move the bad-debt mark holds around $705K and per-share backing holds near 77%. It does not grind to zero.

The cheap part is over, but the meter keeps running. On the slice it cleared the mechanism was efficient: about $8,412 of emissions against $52,314 retired, roughly $0.16 of subsidy per dollar, far under the dollar-for-dollar a direct buyout costs. That efficiency does not extend, because there is nothing left for it to clear cheaply. Meanwhile the DAO keeps paying ~$110K a year in emissions for a pool whose liquidation engine has stalled. The spend still buys something real, just narrower than "recovery": standing exit liquidity for any lender who wants out, at the ~38% haircut to face from goal 1, plus a live venue for the CRV-recovery option.

Lenders mostly just wait. With the grind stalled, a holder's backing sits near 77% rather than climbing toward 100%. The only way to turn a position into cash is to sell into the pool at the ~38% discount, which two lenders have done. Everyone else holds an impaired claim whose value now moves with the CRV price, not with liquidator activity.

The remaining 93% is a one-sided CRV-price bet. The bad debt's exposure to CRV is asymmetric, which is the one piece of good news. On the downside there is little left to lose: these positions are already soft-liquidated through their LLAMMA bands, so what backs them is crvUSD now, not CRV, and a further fall in CRV barely moves the number. On the upside there is real optionality. If CRV climbs back toward the $0.957 to $1.242 band that michwill's recovery analysis marks out in the proposal, the soft-liquidation reverses, the collateral reconverts to CRV at higher value, and those deep positions become solvent and liquidatable on their own. So the constant-price case is the floor of a one-sided bet: the hole can't get much deeper, and only a CRV recovery fills it. With CRV at $0.21 against a band that starts at $0.957, that recovery is far off and entirely exogenous to the mechanism.

6. Can it scale to the other markets?

This is the question that decides whether the recovery pool is a one-off patch or a repeatable tool. The other impaired LlamaLend markets carry roughly $1.22M of bad debt between them, on top of this market's ~$705K.

The cash cost of copying the mechanism is not the binding constraint, because the DAO never pays the principal; liquidators and exiting lenders do. What the DAO spends is gauge weight, and that is the scarce resource. This market uses about 0.45% of weekly CRV emissions to keep ~$705K of debt liquid. Covering the other markets at a similar ratio would take roughly another 0.35% of total weight. In cash terms that's cheap. In governance terms it's contested, because every basis point of weight is taken from some other gauge that wants it, and the affected lenders don't control the vote.

But this market exposed a limit more fundamental than the vote. The arb only reaches debt whose shortfall is shallower than the pool discount, and in the CRV market that turned out to be a single position, about 7% of the hole. The other 93% was too deeply underwater to touch at all. So the question that decides whether the mechanism helps a given market isn't its total bad debt, or even its vote cost. It's how much of that debt sits below the discount ceiling. A market whose loans are mostly deep underwater gets almost nothing from this, no matter how much emissions it's handed. The rented-depth warning still applies on top: each new market carries its own ongoing emissions bill, not a one-time grant.

So the honest read on scalability: the cash cost is trivial, but the mechanism only recovers the shallow, nearly-solvent slice of each market's bad debt. For the deep majority it offers a discounted exit and a standing option on price, not a recovery. Whether a gauge per market is worth it depends on how much of that market is shallow enough to clear, and on a vote that competes with everything else for emissions. It's a narrow tool, not a general one.

7. Bottom line

The pool does the hard part it was designed for. It turns an unworkable loss into a profitable trade, and on the one position where that trade was solvent it cleared ~$52K of bad debt for about $8K of emissions, a fifth of what a buyout would cost. LPs showed up for the yield and made the pool genuinely deep.

But the reach is narrow, and that's the real finding. That $52K is about 7% of the hole, and it's most of what the arb can touch: the other 93% sits in positions too deeply underwater to liquidate at a profit, so at today's CRV price the cleanup has stalled, not slowed. The other goals are thin too. Lenders have no real cash exit, two have used it at a ~38% haircut to face. The option-style speculation didn't materialize; the pool runs on yield-farming LPs and one arbitrage bot, not on people holding the recovery bet. And the depth is rented: fees cover about a third of the emissions, so the DAO keeps paying ~$110K a year to keep a stalled engine warm.

The rest of the bad debt, here and across the other markets, isn't waiting on time or on more liquidators. It's waiting on CRV to recover, which the mechanism can't manufacture.
