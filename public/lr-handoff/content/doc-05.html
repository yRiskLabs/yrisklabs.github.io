Introduction

This document outlines our framework for asset onboarding and parameterization for LlamaLend V2. Our approach combines quantitative and qualitative analysis to form a recommendation on whether an asset can be safely onboarded and under what parameters. We place a key focus on qualitative metrics and quantiative metrics.

The framework begins with a review of the (1) Asset Fundamental Characteristics. Subsequently, the risk assessment is divided into three categories to form a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed asset's overall risk profile: (2) Market Risk, (3) Technological Risk, and (4) Counterparty Risk.

Based on this assessment, (5) Specific Parameters are proposed. Our goal is to conduct a thorough analysis, verify claims, and present our findings in a timely and digestible manner for the DAO stakeholders to make informed decisions.

Depending on the asset under review, the structure of our reports might slightly change.

5. LlamaLend V2 Specific Parameters

This section outlines the specific parameters for LlamaLend V2 that need to be configured when onboarding a new asset or adjusted following changes in its risk profile. These parameters are crucial for defining the asset's behavior within the LlamaLend protocol, including its borrowing and collateral capabilities, risk management settings, and interest rate model.

5. LlamaLend-Specific Parameters

This section pairs the collateral assessment with concrete LlamaLend market configuration based on quantative analysis. Unlike Aave-style lending, risk in LlamaLend is not primarily governed by a single hard liquidation threshold. Instead, market safety is determined by the interaction between the oracle design, LLAMMA band structure, fee settings, discount parameters, exposure limits, and the monetary policy applied to the market. The purpose of this section is therefore to justify not only whether the asset is suitable as collateral, but also under what exact market configuration it can be onboarded safely.

5.1 Oracle Design

The oracle assessment should document the full pricing path used by the market rather than only the top-level source. For each candidate oracle, the report should specify the exact contracts and dependencies used to derive the collateral price against the borrowed asset, including the composition of any underlying Curve pool, the relevant coin indices, any chaining through vault redemption rates, and whether an aggregated crvUSD price is incorporated. This matters because Curve lending oracle contracts are immutable once deployed; the pool composition cannot later be changed inside the oracle contract, so errors in oracle construction require a redeloyment.

5.1.1 Curve Pool Oracles

For Curve-pool-based oracles, the assessment should cover:

  1. Pool composition,

    1. List pool addresses, deployment chains and trade pair

    2. How are pools chained together to achieve the desired collateral and debt pairing?

    3. Are there any assumptions made in price feed composition (e.g., are we equating 1USDC = 1 USD)?

  2. Pool depth and reserve imbalance,

  3. TVL stability,

    1. LP concentration

    2. incentive dependence

  4. Liquidation Routing Dependencies, i.e. is the risk exacbberated as liquidation are primarily trigger in the oracle pool.

  5. Suitability of the pool for long-term price discovery under stress.

  6. Document or decide on smoothing

If the price relies on a vault wrapper (e.g., PPS for yield-bearing) or , the report should additionally assess the vault redemption mechanism and whether the redemption rate can be manipulated or drift faster than is safely captured by the oracle logic.

5.1.2 Chainlink-based Oracles

For Chainlink-based oracles, the assessment should cover:

  1. Feed Composition

    1. List feed deployment addresses, deployment chains and trade pair and heartbeat / deviation configuration

    2. How are feeds chained together to achieve the desired collateral and debt pairing?

    3. Are there any assumptions made in price feed composition (e.g., are we equating 1 USDC = 1 USD)?

  2. Decide whether smoothing is necessary

5.1.3 Other Oracles

For other oracle constructions, the burden of proof should be higher. The report should explain why the oracle is needed, why more standard Curve or Chainlink constructions are insufficient, and what assumptions must hold for the oracle to remain robust in stressed conditions. In general we try to assess Robustness and Sustainability.

5.2 Market Parameters

The core market parameters for LlamaLend are the oracle smoothing choice, A, fee, loan_discount, and liquidation_discount. These should be analyzed jointly using simulation using the LlammaSimulator Framework. In Curve’s controller logic, the user’s maximum borrowable debt depends on collateral amount, oracle price, and the number of bands N, while the effective LTV decreases as the number of bands rises; the relationship between A and N is therefore central to the market’s leverage profile.

  • Oracle smoothing / Texp: This parameter governs how quickly the oracle responds to price moves. A shorter window improves responsiveness but increases sensitivity to transient dislocations; a longer window reduces noise but can delay recognition of real market moves. The recommended value should therefore be justified by empirical comparison of responsiveness versus stability, ideally using historical stress windows and needs to be determined before starting to work with the LlammaSimulator in Section 5.1.

  • A (amplification / band geometry) A affects how collateral is distributed across bands and therefore how the position transitions through soft liquidation. It should be set jointly with the expected borrower behavior and desired leverage profile. Higher effective leverage is not automatically better; it must be supported by oracle quality, executable liquidity, and tolerance for conversion losses in stressed conditions.

  • fee: The LLAMMA fee affects the path of collateral-to-debt conversion and therefore both user outcomes and liquidation dynamics. The fee should be high enough to compensate for adverse selection and repeated trading through the bands, but not so high that it creates unnecessary borrower losses or distorts normal deleveraging.

  • loan_discount: This is the primary parameter limiting initial leverage. It should be calibrated as a protection against both market volatility and recursive looping risk. Assets that can be borrowed against themselves or against closely related wrappers require stricter treatment here.

  • liquidation_discount: This parameter should be justified from executable liquidation conditions rather than from abstract volatility alone. It must be large enough that a liquidator can unwind risk profitably after accounting for price impact, fees, gas, bridge friction where relevant, and oracle-to-execution slippage. The appropriate benchmark is not theoretical spot liquidity, but stressed executable liquidity.

For exact experiment configuration please refer to the LlammaSimulator Documentation.

5.3 Exposure Limits

The main control for exposure is the debt ceiling / borrow cap, which should be calibrated from liquidation capacity under stress. The cap should reflect how much debt can be safely supported if multiple large positions enter soft liquidation and collateral must be converted through available on-chain liquidity. Please utilize the internal Exposure methodology.

5.4 Monetary Policy

There are currently three distinct policy families. As an analyst, you must select one of the following and outline the reasoning appropriately.

  1. Semilog Monetary Policy: a pure utilization-based curve with only a floor and a ceiling. Best suited for volatile tokens.

  2. Secondary Monetary Policy: a utilization-based curve anchored to an external/base rate.

  3. EMA-following Secondary Policy: same Secondary structure, but the base rate is not AMM.rate(); it is an EMA of an external yield source. Best suited for yield-bearing assets with an underlying rate.

Refer to the simulator tools here:

  • Semi-Log IRM App: https://semilogirmapp.streamlit.app/

  • crvUSD Monetary Policy App: https://crvusd-monetary-policy.streamlit.app/

  • Piecewise Linear IRM App: https://piecewiselinearirm.streamlit.app/

  • Secondary Monetary Policy App: https://secondarymp.streamlit.app/

Option 1: Semilog Monetary Policy

Semilog is the simplest policy. The borrow rate depends only on utilization, moving smoothly from a minimum rate at 0% utilization to a maximum rate at 100% utilization. There is no target utilization and no external reference rate; the market is governed entirely by its own utilization state. The interpolation is geometric, so the curve is linear in log-rate space rather than linear in rate space.

Rate formula:

r(u)=rmin​(rmin​rmax​​)u

where u∈[0,1] is utilization, rmin​ is the rate at zero utilization, and rmax​ is the rate at full utilization. Rate values are stored in 1e18-scaled per-second units and are not annualized; borrow APR is obtained by multiplying by 365⋅86400/1018.

Externally set parameters. The only economic parameters are:

  • min_rate: the minimum borrow rate, charged when utilization is 0.

  • max_rate: the maximum borrow rate, charged when utilization is 1.

Both are set at deployment and can later be changed by the factory admin. The implementation enforces min_rate >= MIN_RATE, max_rate <= MAX_RATE, and min_rate <= max_rate; the docs give MIN_RATE = 31709791 and MAX_RATE = 317097919837. ([Curve Knowledge Hub][2])

Parameter intuition: min_rate is the idle-market floor. It determines how cheap borrowing is when liquidity is abundant. max_rate is the scarcity ceiling. It determines how punitive the market becomes as utilization approaches 100%, so it is the main brake against reserve exhaustion.


Option 2: Secondary Monetary Policy

Secondary Monetary Policy is a hyperbolic utilization policy around a reference rate. The design goal is: at a chosen target utilization, the lending-market rate should line up with the base market rate, while at lower or higher utilization it should trade at a discount or premium relative to that base rate.

Rate formula.

r(u)=r0​(rminf​+uinf​−uA​)+shift

where r0​ is the base rate (AMM.rate()), u is utilization, and uinf​,A,rminf​ are derived from the governance inputs. Rates are again 1e18-scaled per-second values and not annualized.

Externally set parameters. The economic inputs are:

  • target_utilization (u0​): the utilization at which the policy is calibrated to match the base rate.

  • low_ratio (α): the ratio of lending-market rate to base rate at 0% utilization.

  • high_ratio (β): the ratio of lending-market rate to base rate at 100% utilization.

  • rate_shift: an additive shift applied to the whole curve.

The implementation bounds shown in the docs/code are: target_utilization between 1% and 99%, low_ratio >= 1e16, high_ratio <= 100e18, low_ratio < high_ratio, and rate_shift <= 100e18.

Parameter intuition. target_utilization is the center of the policy: it defines the utilization level you consider normal or desired. low_ratio controls how cheap the market should be when underutilized; smaller values make borrowing much cheaper than the base market in slack conditions. high_ratio controls how expensive the market should become when utilization is extreme; larger values create a stronger scarcity premium relative to the base rate. rate_shift is a fixed additive spread on top of the whole curve. In practice, that means low_ratio and high_ratio control the multiplicative shape relative to the base rate, while rate_shift adds an absolute spread.


Option 3: EMA-following Secondary Policy

This is structurally a Secondary Monetary Policy with a different base-rate source. Instead of using AMM.rate() as the anchor, it computes a raw rate from the underlying yield-bearing vault and then smooths it with an EMA. The final borrow-rate formula keeps the same hyperbolic utilization overlay as the Secondary, but the reference rate is an EMA of yield. That means the market becomes anchored to the collateral yield regime.

Rate source: To deploy the EMA-smooth Secondary Policy the contract first needs to computes a raw per-second reference rate rraw​ from some underlying rate source. The exact construction depends on the asset or protocol being referenced, but in general this is a rate inferred from underlying state variables and scaled to 1018. If needed, it can be annualized by multiplying by 365⋅86400.

The raw rate is then smoothed with an exponential moving average (EMA):

αt​=e−Δt/TEXPrEMA​=(1−αt​),rnew​+αt​,rold​

where TEXP is the EMA time constant and Δt is the elapsed time since the previous update. A larger TEXP makes the anchor slower and smoother; a smaller TEXP makes it more reactive.

The final monetary-policy rate is then obtained by applying the standard Secondary-style utilization adjustment to this EMA-smoothed anchor:

r(u)=rEMA​(rminf​+uinf​−uA​)+shift

where u is utilization, and rminf​, A, uinf​, and shift are the derived policy parameters. In other words, this is the same structure as Secondary Monetary Policy, except that the external/base rate r0​ is replaced by an EMA-smoothed reference rate.

Externally set parameters. The tunable economic inputs are the same four as Secondary:

  • target_utilization

  • low_ratio

  • high_ratio

  • rate_shift

The same parameter bounds are enforced as in Secondary: utilization between 1% and 99%, low_ratio at least 1%, high_ratio bounded above, low_ratio < high_ratio, and rate_shift bounded above.

In addition there is the smoothing in the external yield rate:

  • TEXP

Parameter intuition: target_utilization, low_ratio, high_ratio, and rate_shift have exactly the same meaning as in Secondary. A larger TEXP means more smoothing and slower adaptation to changes in the yield rate; a smaller TEXP means faster tracking but also more sensitivity to noisy or discontinuous vault-rate updates.
