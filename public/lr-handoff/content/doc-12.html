Methodology: Simulation-Based Interest Rate Model Optimization

This section describes a reproducible methodology for optimizing interest rate model parameters in decentralized lending markets. The objective is to identify interest rate model parameterizations that stabilize market utilization around a target level while controlling undesirable policy outcomes such as persistent over-utilization, persistent under-utilization, excessive utilization volatility, and excessive borrow-rate volatility. The framework combines empirical market data, liquidity-based target-utilization estimation, regime-window selection, reduced-form utilization modeling, Monte Carlo simulation, and simulation-based policy optimization.

Let ut​ denote market utilization at time t, rt​ the borrow rate implied by a candidate interest rate model, pt​ the oracle price of the relevant collateral or market reference asset, and At​ the total asset base of the lending market. The central policy problem is to choose an interest rate model parameter vector θ such that the induced borrow-rate path rt​(θ) guides utilization toward a target u∗, while avoiding excessive instability in both utilization and rates.

The methodology is implemented at the individual market level. For each market, historical data are transformed into a standardized time-series dataset containing, at minimum, timestamps, utilization, borrow rates, oracle prices, and total market assets. This dataset is the empirical input for all subsequent estimation, calibration, simulation, and optimization stages. All final outputs are therefore conditional on the quality, frequency, and completeness of the underlying market data.

Market Data and Preprocessing

For each lending market, the data-ingestion stage constructs a cleaned market-level dataframe. The required market identifiers include the chain, controller address, market launch date, and output path. The launch date is used to truncate observations before the market became economically meaningful, while the controller address and chain identify the market whose utilization and liquidity dynamics are being analyzed.

The resulting dataframe is treated as the canonical empirical object for the experiment. It is used to estimate the utilization target, identify the calibration regime, fit utilization dynamics, initialize simulations, and generate reporting artifacts. To ensure reproducibility, both the raw data extract and the processed dataframe should be archived. This is particularly important because repeated data retrievals may differ over time due to API backfills, indexing changes, or historical data corrections.

Optimal Utilization Estimation

The utilization target is estimated from historical liquidity stress rather than imposed exogenously. The method interprets optimal utilization as the highest utilization level that would have retained sufficient unutilized liquidity to absorb historically observed withdrawals during volatile oracle-price periods. The intuition is that a lending market should not target a utilization level so high that historically plausible stress withdrawals would exhaust available liquidity.

Oracle-price changes are first computed as percentage returns:

rtp​=100(pt−1​pt​​−1).

A volatility threshold is then defined as:

τp​=κ⋅σ(rtp​),

where $$\kappa$$ is the configured volatility multiplier, denoted in implementation as std_multiplier, and $$\sigma(r^p_t)$$ is the sample standard deviation of oracle-price returns. An observation is classified as a volatile market state if:

∣rtp​∣>τp​.

The method then measures percentage changes in the total market asset base:

rtA​=100(At−1​At​​−1).

Only observations that satisfy two conditions are retained for the stress-withdrawal sample: the observation must occur during a volatile oracle-price period, and the corresponding change in total assets must be negative. The stress-withdrawal sample is therefore defined as:

S={rtA​:∣rtp​∣>τp​ and rtA​<0}.

The withdrawal stress estimate is obtained from the empirical lower-tail quantile of this restricted sample. For a configured withdrawal quantile $$\alpha$$, define:

qα​=Qα​(S),

where Qα​ denotes the empirical α-quantile. Since S contains only negative asset-base changes, qα​<0. The liquidity-compatible utilization target implied by this stress withdrawal is:

u~∗=1+100qα​​.

This transformation converts an observed stress withdrawal into a required unutilized liquidity reserve. For example, if the selected stress-withdrawal quantile is −12, then the market would require approximately 12 unutilized liquidity to absorb such a withdrawal, implying u~∗=0.88.

Finally, the target is capped by a configurable growth buffer g:

u∗=min(g,u~∗).

The growth buffer prevents the target from becoming too aggressive in markets where observed withdrawal stress is small or where the historical sample does not contain sufficiently severe liquidity events. The resulting u∗ is therefore a stress-liquidity utilization target: it represents the highest utilization level compatible with the selected empirical withdrawal quantile during volatile oracle-price states, subject to an imposed upper cap.

For reproducibility, each market-level application should report the oracle-price return standard deviation, the volatility threshold τp​, the number of volatile observations, the number of negative-withdrawal observations during volatile periods, the full withdrawal-quantile table, the selected quantile qα​, the growth buffer g, and the final target u∗.

If no negative-withdrawal observations occur during volatile oracle-price states, the method cannot estimate u∗ from the restricted stress-withdrawal sample. In that case, a fallback rule must be specified before optimization is performed. Possible fallback choices include using the lower-tail withdrawal quantile from the full sample, imposing a conservative externally specified utilization cap, or excluding the market from optimization due to insufficient stress-withdrawal evidence.

Regime-Window Selection

After estimating the utilization target, the method selects the empirical calibration window used for model fitting and simulation. This step is necessary because lending markets may experience structural changes in utilization, liquidity composition, borrower behavior, or policy conditions. Estimating utilization dynamics over the full historical sample may combine periods that are not representative of the current market state.

The regime selector is implemented as a rolling z-score persistence rule applied to a configured market variable, typically utilization. Let $$x_t$$ denote the selected regime variable. In the default setting:

xt​=ut​.

For a rolling window of length $$W$$, the local mean and standard deviation are:

μt(W)​=W1​i=t−W+1∑t​xi​,

and:

σt(W)​=W−11​i=t−W+1∑t​(xi​−μt(W)​)2​.

The rolling z-score is then:

zt​=σt(W)​xt​−μt(W)​​.

Early observations for which the rolling window is incomplete are not treated as regime shifts. In implementation, missing early-window z-scores are set to zero. An observation is classified as an outlier if:

∣zt​∣>zthreshold​,

where $$z_{\text{threshold}}$$ is the configured absolute z-score boundary.

A single outlier is not sufficient to define a regime shift. Instead, the method requires persistence. Let:

It​=1(∣zt​∣>zthreshold​)

be the outlier indicator. A regime shift is detected at time $$t$$ if the rolling sum of outlier indicators over the persistence window $$C$$ equals $$C$$:

j=t−C+1∑t​Ij​=C,

where C is the configured persistence parameter, implemented as consecutive days. Thus, isolated utilization spikes are treated as noise, while sustained deviations are interpreted as evidence of a new empirical regime.

The detected shift timestamps are used as candidate boundaries between empirical regimes. The calibration window is selected as the most recent stable interval satisfying a minimum duration requirement. If the interval from the most recent detected shift to the end of the dataset is at least d days, that interval is selected. If no interval satisfies the minimum duration requirement, the detector returns no stable regime and the market should not proceed to model fitting without a documented manual override.

Let the ordered set of detected regime-shift timestamps be:

T=T1​,T2​,…,TK​,

where TK​ is the most recent detected shift and Tend​ is the final timestamp in the dataset. The preferred calibration interval is:

[TK​,Tend​],

provided that:

Tend​−TK​≥d days.

If this condition is not satisfied, the algorithm fails. The resulting interval [T0​,T1​] defines the sample used for fitting the utilization-dynamics model and initializing the simulation environment. If manual analysis start date or analysis end date values are specified, these values override the automatically selected boundaries. However, both the automatically selected regime window and the manually overridden window should be reported.

The regime-selection procedure should be interpreted as a deterministic calibration-window selection rule rather than a structural model of market regimes. Its output depends on the selected variable, rolling window length, z-score threshold, persistence requirement, observation frequency, and minimum stable-window duration. Consequently, sensitivity analysis over these parameters is required when optimized interest rate model recommendations are materially affected by the selected calibration window.

Utilization-Dynamics Model

Within the selected calibration window, the framework estimates a reduced-form model of utilization dynamics. The dependent variable is typically the change in utilization:

Δut​=ut​−ut−1​.

The explanatory variables may include lagged utilization, lagged borrow rates, rate changes, or other market-specific drivers. The general model can be written as:

Δut​=α+Xt​β+εt​,

where Xt​ is the vector of explanatory variables, β is the corresponding coefficient vector, and εt​ is the residual term.

The purpose of this model is not to estimate a complete structural model of borrower and supplier behavior. Instead, it provides a reduced-form approximation of utilization dynamics that can be embedded in a simulation environment. The estimated relationship determines how simulated utilization evolves in response to market conditions and policy-relevant rate variables.

Model fitting is subject to several quality-control constraints. These include outlier filtering, minimum sample-size requirements, coefficient-significance thresholds, explanatory-power thresholds, and constraints on residual uncertainty. These safeguards reduce the risk that weak, noisy, or economically implausible empirical relationships are passed directly into the policy-optimization stage.

The fitted model should be assessed statistically and economically. Statistical diagnostics include sample size, coefficient significance, explanatory power, residual variance, and confidence intervals. Economic diagnostics include the sign and magnitude of the estimated rate response, the stability of coefficients across calibration windows, and whether the implied utilization dynamics are plausible under market stress.

Instrumental-Variable Validation

Where identification of the rate-utilization relationship is questionable, the framework may be run in an instrumental-variable validation mode. In this mode, the procedure estimates and stores econometric diagnostics but does not proceed to interest rate model optimization.

This validation step is intended to address the possibility that borrow rates are endogenous to utilization. In lending markets, rates are often mechanically determined by utilization through the interest rate model itself, while utilization is simultaneously affected by rates. A naive regression of utilization changes on borrow rates may therefore suffer from simultaneity or reverse-causality bias.

The instrumental-variable validation procedure applies weak-instrument safeguards. These include thresholds on first-stage strength, statistical significance, and partial explanatory power. In the implementation, the relevant thresholds include a first-stage F-statistic threshold, a weak-instrument p-value threshold, and a partial R2 threshold. If the instrument is weak or the fitted relationship is not statistically credible, the resulting utilization response should not be used as the basis for parameter optimization.

This separation between econometric validation and policy optimization is important. A simulation-based optimizer can otherwise produce precise-looking parameter recommendations even when the underlying empirical relationship is weakly identified.

Simulation Design

After fitting the utilization-dynamics model, the framework evaluates candidate interest rate model policies through Monte Carlo simulation. For each candidate parameter vector $$\theta$$, future utilization paths are simulated over a fixed horizon. At each simulated step, the candidate interest rate model maps utilization into a borrow rate:

rt​=fθ​(ut​),

where fθ​(⋅) denotes the interest rate model function parameterized by θ. The fitted utilization-dynamics model then updates utilization according to the estimated response process.

In simplified form, the simulated utilization process can be written as:

Δut+1​=α^+Xt​(θ)β^​+ε^t+1​,

and:

ut+1​=ut​+Δut+1​.

Here, Xt​(θ) may include variables affected by the candidate interest rate model, such as the borrow rate or rate change. The residual term ε^t+1​ captures stochastic variation around the fitted dynamics. Depending on the configuration, the simulation may include uncertainty propagation, residual variance sampling, and repeated path generation.

This creates a closed-loop policy evaluation environment. Candidate interest rate model parameters affect simulated rates, simulated rates affect utilization dynamics, and the resulting utilization paths determine the performance metrics used for optimization.

The simulation is controlled by parameters such as the number of simulated paths, number of time steps, analysis start and end dates, uncertainty-propagation settings, residual sampling settings, and the deviation tolerance around the target utilization. These parameters must be reported because they affect the stability and interpretation of the optimization results.

Policy Evaluation Metrics

Candidate interest rate model policies are evaluated using several metrics computed over simulated paths. The principal metric is the mean squared deviation of utilization from the target:

MSE=NT1​n=1∑N​t=1∑T​(un,t​−u∗)2,

where N is the number of simulated paths and T is the number of simulated time steps.

The method also evaluates the amount of time utilization spends above or below acceptable tolerance bands around u∗. Let $$\delta$$ denote the configured deviation tolerance. The upper and lower utilization bands are:

uupper=u∗+δ,

and:

ulower=u∗−δ.

The fraction of simulated time spent above the upper band is:

TimeAbove=NT1​n=1∑N​t=1∑T​1(un,t​>uupper).

Similarly, the fraction of simulated time spent below the lower band is:

TimeBelow=NT1​n=1∑N​t=1∑T​1(un,t​<ulower).

The framework also measures utilization volatility and rate volatility. These metrics penalize policies that achieve target tracking only by creating unstable utilization or unstable borrower-facing rates.

Optimization Objective

The optimization objective is a weighted aggregate of the simulation metrics. For a candidate interest rate model parameter vector θ, the objective is:

L(θ)=w1​MSE+w2​TimeAbove+w3​TimeBelow+w4​Volatility(ut​)+w5​Volatility(rt​),

where w1​,…,w5​ are researcher-specified weights.

These weights encode normative policy preferences. A higher weight on TimeAbove penalizes policies that frequently produce high-utilization liquidity stress. A higher weight on TimeBelow penalizes policies that leave the market persistently underutilized. A higher weight on rate volatility penalizes unstable borrower-facing rates. The optimized parameter vector is therefore conditional not only on the data and fitted utilization model, but also on the selected policy preferences.

The optimization problem is:

θ∗=argθ∈Θmin​L(θ),

where Θ is the feasible parameter space for the interest rate model class under consideration.

The framework supports multiple interest rate model classes. Each candidate model is defined by its parameter bounds, fixed variables, and optional market-specific placeholders. Some parameter values or bounds may be expressed relative to $$u^\ast$$, allowing the same model specification to adapt across markets while preserving a common policy structure.

The optimizer searches over the feasible parameter space using configured global-optimization settings, including maximum iterations, population size, restart behavior, convergence tolerances, fixed variables, and random seeds. Since global optimization may be sensitive to initialization and stochastic search behavior, random seeds and restart settings should be fixed and reported.

Outputs and Reporting

The final output consists of optimized interest rate model parameter vectors, comparative performance metrics, utilization-path diagnostics, regime-selection outputs, model-fitting diagnostics, simulation summaries, and report artifacts.

A reproducible empirical run should archive the full configuration file, code revision, exact run command, raw input data, processed dataframe, estimated $$u^\ast$$, selected regime window, fitted model diagnostics, instrumental-variable diagnostics where applicable, optimizer settings, random seed, optimized parameter vectors, and final metric tables.

For publication or independent replication, each market-level result should report at minimum:

  1. the market identifier, data frequency, and observation period;

  2. the optimal-utilization parameters and resulting u∗;

  3. the volatility threshold and stress-withdrawal sample size used to estimate u∗;

  4. the regime-detection parameters and selected calibration window;

  5. the utilization-dynamics model specification and diagnostics;

  6. any instrumental-variable diagnostics;

  7. the simulation configuration;

  8. the objective weights;

  9. the optimized interest rate model parameters;

  10. comparative performance metrics across candidate model classes.

Interpretation

The output of the framework should be interpreted as a conditional policy recommendation. It answers the following question: given the selected market data, utilization-target heuristic, calibration window, fitted utilization dynamics, simulation design, objective weights, and optimizer configuration, which interest rate model parameterization performs best according to the specified evaluation criteria?

The method does not identify a universally optimal interest rate model. The result is conditional on assumptions about stress withdrawals, regime stability, reduced-form utilization dynamics, simulation uncertainty, and policy preferences. Consequently, optimized parameters should be treated as decision-support outputs requiring economic review and robustness analysis before being used in production or governance recommendations.

Threats to Validity

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the utilization target depends on historical stress withdrawals. If the historical sample does not contain relevant stress events, the estimated $$u^\ast$$ may be too aggressive. Conversely, if the sample contains idiosyncratic one-off withdrawals, the target may be overly conservative.

Second, the regime-selection procedure is a deterministic rolling-threshold rule rather than a structural model of market regimes. Its output may change materially with the rolling window length, z-score threshold, persistence requirement, or observation frequency.

Third, the utilization-dynamics model is reduced-form. It may capture empirical correlations without identifying stable causal responses. This is especially relevant when borrow rates are mechanically linked to utilization through the existing interest rate model.

Fourth, the simulation environment inherits the assumptions and weaknesses of the fitted model. If the fitted process fails to capture nonlinear behavior, structural breaks, jumps, liquidity shocks, or borrower coordination, the simulated paths may understate true policy risk.

Fifth, the optimization objective depends on researcher-specified weights. These weights are not estimated from the data; they encode policy preferences. Therefore, sensitivity analysis over objective weights is required to determine whether recommendations are robust.

Finally, global optimization may be sensitive to parameter bounds, random seeds, and restart behavior. Exact reproducibility requires fixed seeds, archived configurations, and stored intermediate artifacts.

For these reasons, the methodology should be used as a transparent and reproducible framework for interest rate model parameter discovery, not as a fully autonomous parameter-setting mechanism.
